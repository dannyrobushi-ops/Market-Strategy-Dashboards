The VIX Is Missing The Single-Stock Tape

Index volatility is calm, but the single-stock tape is not. Constituent volatility and dispersion are pressing higher while implied correlation is near the floor, which means risk is moving under the index rather than through it.

Key Conclusions

  • VIX is 16.14 and sits near the 28% one-year percentile, so index-level volatility is not signaling broad stress.
  • VIXEQ is 50.01 at the 100% one-year percentile, which says single-stock option volatility is already hot.
  • DSPX is 47.15 at the 100% one-year percentile, while COR1M is only 5.27 at the 1.6% percentile.
  • The market-structure risk is not low volatility by itself. It is high single-stock volatility plus very low correlation, because VIX can reprice quickly if stocks start moving together.
Index Map

What The Volatility Indexes Are Measuring

VIX is the market's 30-day implied volatility estimate for the S&P 500 index. It is an index-volatility gauge, not a direct measure of how volatile the individual stocks inside the index are becoming.

VIXEQ is the S&P 500 constituent-volatility index. It looks through the index and measures the implied-volatility tape across the underlying stocks. When VIXEQ rises while VIX stays calm, single-stock risk is heating up without yet becoming a broad index-volatility event.

DSPX is the S&P 500 dispersion index. It captures the expected gap between stock-level movement and index-level movement. High dispersion means stock selection, relative value, and idiosyncratic risk matter more.

COR1M is the one-month implied-correlation index. Low correlation means stocks are not being priced to move together. That can suppress the VIX because winners and losers offset inside the index. The dangerous transition is when correlation begins rising while constituent volatility and dispersion are already high.

The broader option-surface indexes in the deck add more texture. SKEW tracks far-left-tail pricing, LTV tracks left-tail stress, SMILE tracks the shape of the option volatility surface, and BFLY tracks range-premium structure. They are useful second-level checks, but the core message here is the VIX/VIXEQ/DSPX/COR1M split.

Volatility Regime

The Headline VIX Is Too Narrow

The volatility tape is sending two different messages. The VIX, which measures 30-day implied volatility on the S&P 500 index, is still calm. That is why the surface looks orderly at the index level. But the underlying single-stock tape is not calm. The constituent-volatility index is at the top of its one-year range, and dispersion is also at the top of its one-year range.

That distinction matters. The VIX is an index-volatility measure. It falls when the market expects the S&P 500 to move less as a single basket. VIXEQ and DSPX look underneath that basket. They ask whether the individual stocks inside the index are becoming more volatile and more differentiated. Today, the answer is yes.

Quick Take

Low VIX is not the same thing as low risk. It can also mean the index is being held together by offsetting single-stock moves while the underlying equity tape becomes more unstable.

Dispersion

The Market Is Moving More By Stock Than By Index

Dispersion is the expected spread between single-stock movement and index movement. When dispersion rises, stock selection matters more because the market is rewarding and punishing individual names more aggressively. That can be bullish for active managers and relative-value books, but it also means the index is not telling the full risk story.

The other side of that setup is correlation. One-month implied correlation is very low. Low correlation means the market is not moving as one block. It suppresses index volatility because gains in one set of stocks can offset losses in another. The danger is that correlation is not permanently low. If macro risk forces stocks to move together while single-stock volatility is already elevated, the VIX can catch up fast.

Risk Read

This Is A Fragile Calm, Not A Clean Sell Signal

This is not automatically bearish. A market can grind higher with high dispersion if leadership rotates, losers remain contained, and correlation stays low. In that world the index remains orderly, option sellers can keep collecting index premium, and stock-specific opportunities stay rich.

The risk case is different. If high dispersion begins to meet rising correlation, the same single-stock volatility that looked idiosyncratic can become index volatility. That is when a calm VIX becomes less useful as a standalone risk gauge. The handoff to watch is not simply VIX up or down; it is whether constituent volatility remains elevated while correlation stops falling.

Bottom Line

The market is not pricing a broad volatility event, but it is pricing more single-stock instability. That is a stock-pickers' tape until correlation turns higher; after that, it becomes an index-risk tape.
