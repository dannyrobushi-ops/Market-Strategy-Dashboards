The AI Boom Is Becoming A Policy Constraint

The June FOMC minutes put the AI infrastructure cycle into the policy debate. Capex is still real, but the macro read is no longer just productivity and earnings. If data-center investment keeps power demand and core inflation sticky while growth slows, the Fed can stay patient even without needing to hike.

Key Conclusions

  • Our Fed-language score has turned more hawkish, with the latest reading at +2.7. That means recent Fed communications are using more restrictive and inflation-focused language than their recent norm. Five-year breakevens have cooled to 2.24%, but the communication impulse is still more restrictive than the market inflation line alone suggests.
  • The real-economy AI channel is visible in GDP composition: information-processing and software contributed 1.3 pp to real GDP growth in the latest quarter, versus 0.4 pp from personal consumption.
  • Consumer inflation psychology has cooled from the worst point, but it is not back to the old comfort zone: Michigan one-year expectations are 4.6% and five-year expectations are 3.3%.
  • The market is already paying for the physical AI chain. Since April 2024, with each basket indexed to 100 for a clean cumulative-return comparison, the AI supplier basket is 394 and the power/cooling basket is 314, versus QQQ at 161.
Policy

The Fed Is Not Treating AI As Pure Productivity

The important shift in the minutes is not that the Fed suddenly discovered AI. Markets have already priced AI as a growth and margin story. The more interesting point is that the central bank is starting to treat AI infrastructure as a macro impulse that can complicate the disinflation path.

That matters because a productivity story normally gives the market permission to pay higher multiples: stronger real growth, lower unit labor pressure, and eventually a better supply side. But the near-term buildout is also physical. It needs data centers, power, cooling, equipment, land, labor, and financing. Those are real inputs, not software abstractions.

Quick Take

AI can be bullish for earnings and still hawkish for the reaction function. The Fed does not need to hike for that to matter; it only needs enough inflation doubt to delay the easing impulse markets want.

Growth Mix

The AI Channel Is Showing Up In Real Activity

The reason this belongs in a macro note is that AI is no longer only a market narrative. Information-processing and software investment is already contributing to real GDP growth. That does not mean the entire economy is being lifted by AI, but it does mean the capex channel is big enough to show up in the macro accounts.

This is the useful distinction. A productivity boom is ultimately supply-side positive. A capital cycle begins as demand: equipment, structures, software, power, cooling, labor, financing, and grid capacity. Until that spending clearly converts into broad productivity and cash flow, the Fed has to treat it as a demand impulse as well as a potential efficiency story.

Inflation Gate

Expectations Are Still Too High To Ignore

The inflation debate is no longer the 2022 shock, but it is also not back to the pre-pandemic range. The level matters because expectations are the Fed's bridge between one-off price moves and a more persistent inflation process.

That is where the AI buildout becomes macro-relevant. A productivity boom can eventually be disinflationary, but the first stage is physical investment. If households still expect elevated inflation and the infrastructure cycle is adding demand for scarce inputs, the Fed has less incentive to rush the easing cycle.

Market Read

The Market Is Paying For The Physical AI Chain

The equity tape is already separating the AI story into two pieces. The compute suppliers remain the highest-beta expression of the boom, but the power and cooling sleeve has become its own leadership group. That is not just a stock-market curiosity. It is a price signal that the bottleneck is physical.

The clean version of the trade is selective. The market should reward companies where AI capex is already tied to revenue growth, margin durability, and positive revisions. It should be more skeptical of places where the story is mostly capacity, electricity load, or strategic spending without visible payback.

Bottom Line

The AI boom is still real, but it is not a free easing story. The next leg needs proof that capex is becoming productivity and cash flow before the physical buildout becomes another reason for policy to stay restrictive.
