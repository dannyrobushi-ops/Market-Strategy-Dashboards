Breadth Is Improving While Leadership Cools

The broad indexes are still above their long-term trends, but weekly momentum has softened most in the AI-heavy leadership sleeves. The more constructive counterweight is that equal weight and smaller companies are holding up, which keeps the consolidation from looking like a broad market break.

Key Conclusions

  • SPY remains above its 50- and 200-week averages, but its weekly MACD is rolling over. The message is consolidation inside a larger uptrend, not a completed trend break.
  • RSP has stabilized and recovered from its spring low versus SPY while IWM's weekly trend has held up better than QQQ's. That is early, rather than conclusive, evidence that the index can digest concentrated leadership without an indiscriminate withdrawal of risk.
  • Breadth is mixed but improving away from the AI-heavy sleeves: 64.2% of S&P 500 constituents are above their 50-day averages and 65.5% have positive 20-day returns, while QQQ and technology remain materially weaker internally.
  • Iran-related energy risk and a reassessment of AI competitive moats raise the hurdle for the same leaders to carry the index. The next positive test is whether broader participation can persist without a deeper break in earnings or credit.
Market Structure

Momentum Is Cooling, But The Trend Has Not Broken

The equity market is sending a more nuanced signal than the headline index alone suggests. The S&P 500 remains above its 50- and 200-week averages, so the primary trend has not been invalidated, but weekly momentum has rolled over after an extended advance. That matters because a declining MACD at a high level is not the same as a bear-market signal. It says the rate of advance is slowing, which changes the market's dependence on incremental good news. Until recently, the index could absorb higher yields, geopolitical headlines, and a high valuation bar because a narrow group of AI-linked leaders continued to produce upside earnings revisions. A slower momentum profile means that burden is becoming harder for the same group to carry alone. The relevant question is therefore not whether the S&P 500 can hold a particular closing level for a few sessions. It is whether participation broadens enough to offset a more selective tape in the stocks that have done most of the work. That is the distinction between a healthy consolidation and a more consequential loss of market sponsorship.

The macro setting makes that distinction more important. The conflict around Iran and disruption risk around the Strait of Hormuz keep an energy-price tail risk alive at the same time the market is reassessing the durability of AI leadership. Oil-related inflation risk does not have to become a sustained supply shock to matter. A higher energy bill can raise the hurdle for a clean disinflation narrative, keep real yields firmer, and make the market less willing to extend the valuation of long-duration growth assets. At the same time, Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 release has reminded investors that frontier model performance is not confined to the existing U.S. platform leaders. That does not negate the economic value of AI infrastructure or the capex cycle. It does make the distribution of AI profits less certain, particularly where valuations already assume durable scarcity, pricing power, and exceptional earnings delivery. The market implication is a shift from buying the theme indiscriminately to asking which parts of the theme retain pricing power when the technical backdrop is no longer accelerating.

Internal Leadership

Equal Weight Is Carrying More Of The Conversation

The relative-strength chart is the cleaner cross-check on whether this is merely another pause in the index or a change in the market's internal leadership. A rising RSP-to-SPY ratio means the equal-weight S&P 500 is outperforming the capitalization-weighted index. In other words, capital is becoming less dependent on the very largest stocks. The ratio has recovered from its spring low, but it has not yet reversed its broader decline. That distinction matters because the capitalization-weighted index can remain resilient even when its median constituent is not participating, while a sustained recovery in equal weight would signal that returns are beginning to distribute more broadly. The latest breadth data is consistent with an early improvement rather than a complete all-clear: 64.2% of S&P 500 constituents are above their 50-day moving averages and 65.5% have positive 20-day returns, while QQQ has only 26.4% above its 50-day average and technology has only 39.2% with positive 20-day returns. The important observation is not that breadth is uniformly strong. It is that the weakness remains concentrated in the previous leadership complex while financials, staples, utilities, real estate, and selected cyclicals show comparatively better internal repair.

That pattern is constructive only if it becomes durable. A broadening market can absorb a pause in the largest technology names because earnings, credit creation, and capital flows begin finding other outlets. By contrast, a weak RSP-to-SPY ratio alongside falling technology and semiconductor momentum would indicate that the index is losing leadership without finding a credible replacement. The current evidence is still closer to the first case. Small caps have remained technically firmer than the Nasdaq 100 in recent weeks, and equal weight is recovering relative ground, even though the highest-beta parts of the AI trade have become more vulnerable to a reset in expectations. This is why the breadth data matters for portfolio construction. It does not argue for abandoning the AI cycle. It argues for distinguishing the growth of the underlying investment cycle from the price action of the most crowded beneficiaries, while watching whether lower-volatility, value, and smaller-company participation can make the advance less concentrated.

Quick Take

The index is consolidating because leadership is losing momentum. The market remains more constructive than that headline suggests only if RSP, IWM, and improving constituent participation continue to absorb more of the return burden.

Leadership Test

QQQ Is Losing Speed Faster Than Small Caps

The divergence between QQQ and IWM clarifies where the immediate technical weakness is occurring. The Nasdaq 100 has seen weekly momentum deteriorate more quickly as the market has become less willing to pay higher multiples for every AI-linked growth story. That reassessment has several inputs: earnings growth is concentrated in a narrow set of large companies, AI capital spending remains enormous and therefore increasingly subject to questions about return on invested capital, and open-weight Chinese models such as Kimi K3 create a more competitive benchmark for software and platform economics. None of those developments prove that the AI cycle has failed. They do mean that the market can no longer treat infrastructure scarcity, model leadership, and application-layer margins as one homogeneous trade. As correlations inside growth rise during a de-rating episode, the index-level impact can become larger than a single-company narrative would imply. That is why a rolling QQQ MACD deserves attention even while the broader index stays near its highs.

Small caps are not a risk-free alternative, and their durability still depends on nominal growth, lending conditions, and the absence of a renewed rate shock. But their relative technical resilience is useful because it suggests investors are not simply withdrawing from equity risk. Rather, they are becoming more selective about where they are willing to pay for duration and concentration. This is also the cross-asset implication of the current tape. If oil pressure remains contained and the conflict around Iran does not generate a broader inflation shock, a rotation toward equal weight and smaller companies can coexist with a more measured policy path. If energy moves materially higher or inflation expectations become unanchored, the same rotation can become harder to sustain because real yields and financing costs would rise with it. The market implication is that breadth is an active confirmation signal, not a decorative statistic: improving participation would make a technology consolidation healthier, while a renewed collapse in participation would turn the same technical weakness into a larger regime warning.

Bottom Line

A Broader Market Is The Constructive Outcome

The market does not need every leadership stock to keep rising for the broader advance to remain intact. It does, however, need the consolidation in the AI-heavy complex to be offset by stronger participation elsewhere. The constructive path is one in which equal weight, small caps, and sectors with less demanding expectations continue to improve while the largest technology names digest an unusually strong run and a more competitive AI narrative. That would lower concentration risk without requiring a recessionary growth scare. The less constructive path is a synchronized decline in the leaders with no offset from breadth, at the same time that higher oil prices or a worsening Iran conflict tighten the inflation and financial-conditions backdrop. For now, the technical evidence supports the more constructive interpretation, but only provisionally. The market is no longer being carried by momentum alone. Its next test is whether improving breadth can become a durable source of return rather than a temporary rotation during a high-valuation consolidation.
