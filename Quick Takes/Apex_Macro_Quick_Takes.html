CPI Owns The Reaction Function

Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller did not give the market a clean easing signal. He put policy at a crossroads and made the next move conditional on core inflation at a time when labor looks balanced enough to keep the Fed focused on prices.

Key Conclusions

  • Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller framed policy as a crossroads, not a pivot. Softer inflation would justify patience; another hot core reading would keep the tightening option alive.
  • Core inflation remains above target while market inflation compensation is still contained. That combination allows deliberate policy, but it does not validate easier financial conditions by itself.
  • Labor is balanced enough that it does not yet force an easing response. Initial claims and unemployment matter because they define how much inflation pressure the Fed can tolerate.
  • For markets, the CPI release is a financial-conditions event: cooler core inflation can validate duration and breadth; hotter core inflation turns the front end back into the tightening channel.
Policy Crossroads

The Speech Was Not A Pivot

Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller's speech matters because it narrowed the policy debate to a cleaner trade-off. As a member of the Fed's Board of Governors and a permanent FOMC voter, Waller is not just commenting on the data; he is describing the reaction function that can shape the next policy decision. He described an economy that is still expanding, a labor market that has cooled but has not broken, and an inflation backdrop that still sits above the Fed's objective. June payrolls rose only 57,000 and prior months were revised lower, but Waller also noted that the three-month payroll average remains materially stronger than the pace of labor-supply growth seen last year. That is the important market point. The Fed is not yet being forced to choose employment protection over inflation control, which means the next CPI print carries more policy information than a normal monthly release. The policy question is not whether activity has slowed at the margin. It is whether inflation has cooled enough for the Fed to let financial conditions ease without risking another round of price pressure.

Unless I see evidence of a significantly weakening labor market, my focus will be on inflation.

Christopher J. Waller, Federal Reserve, July 13, 2026

The distinction is important for markets because Waller did not say one benign print would be enough. He said he needs several lower core readings before gaining confidence that inflation is moving back toward target, and he kept the tightening path alive if core inflation stays firm. That matters because the Fed does not need to hike immediately for financial conditions to tighten. A hotter CPI print can reprice the front end, lift the dollar, pressure long-duration equity leadership, and weaken breadth before the Committee changes the policy rate. Conversely, a cooler print would not create an aggressive easing cycle by itself, but it would reduce the probability that the next policy surprise is hawkish. The cross-asset implication is that inflation is now the variable deciding whether lower yields are constructive or restrictive. If yields fall because inflation is cooling, duration and equity breadth can both improve. If yields rise because the Fed has to defend the inflation target, the same market that has been leaning on multiple expansion has to absorb a higher discount-rate path.

Quick Take

The market should not read Waller as a rate-cut signal. The cleaner read is that inflation now owns the reaction function because labor is not weak enough to take the decision away from prices.

Reaction Function

Anchored Expectations Are Helpful, Not A Free Pass

Waller's point on inflation expectations is the key bridge between the macro data and asset pricing. Market-based inflation compensation has not behaved like 2022, which gives the Fed room to move more deliberately than it did during the last inflation shock. Waller specifically pointed to short- and intermediate-term market expectations that remain close to target, but he also argued that anchored expectations do not let central bankers simply wait out above-target inflation. That distinction matters because anchored expectations lower the probability of a disorderly policy response, but they do not make a persistent core-inflation problem disappear. If core prices keep running above target, the Fed still has one problem to solve, and policy cannot validate easier financial conditions until the data give it cover. For markets, this is the difference between a benign inflation scare and a renewed tightening impulse. Inflation expectations can stay contained while the front end still reprices higher if realized inflation refuses to confirm the disinflation path.

The inflation mix is why this remains a market-relevant constraint. Waller pointed to tariffs, energy prices, and AI-related investment as channels that can keep price pressure firmer than the market would prefer. Those channels matter because they affect different parts of the price basket in different ways. Goods disinflation can help the headline number, but services inflation and median CPI determine whether the Fed can become more confident that the underlying trend is moving back toward target. If core goods, services, and the median inflation trend do not cool together, the market can get a softer headline print without receiving the policy relief it wants. That is the practical implication for duration and equities. The market is not simply pricing lower inflation. It is pricing lower inflation arriving quickly enough, and broadly enough, for the Fed to avoid leaning against easier financial conditions. This is also where the AI cycle becomes macro-relevant. If AI investment supports earnings but also keeps pressure on equipment, electricity, construction, and selected labor inputs, the same force helping equity leadership can make it harder for the Fed to deliver the discount-rate relief those equities want.

Labor Cushion

Labor Gives The Fed Time, Not Permission

The labor side of Waller's speech was not an afterthought. It is the reason the Fed can keep the inflation gate in charge. Payroll growth has slowed, revisions have mattered, and participation data have created noise, but the broader labor-market signal is still closer to balance than outright stress. Waller emphasized that job openings, hires, separations, and vacancies relative to unemployed workers do not yet point to a disorderly labor break. Initial claims tell the same story. They are not confirming a layoff shock, and the unemployment rate is not rising in the way it normally does when the policy debate shifts from inflation containment to labor-market protection. That matters because the Fed's tolerance for higher rates depends on whether tightening threatens employment quickly enough to change the mandate trade-off. A soft labor market would turn lower yields into a recession signal. A balanced labor market lets lower yields remain a constructive disinflation signal, but only if CPI cooperates. This is why claims deserve more attention than the payroll headline alone. Claims are the bridge between slower hiring and actual layoffs.

For markets, this keeps the risk concentrated in the CPI reaction rather than in a clean growth scare. If claims begin rising and unemployment follows, the Fed's reaction function would change because policy would be tightening into a labor break. That would favor a different cross-asset map: weaker cyclicals, lower yields for the wrong reason, wider credit spreads, and a higher probability that defensive leadership begins to outperform. Today the message is narrower. Labor is soft enough to make additional tightening riskier than in 2022, but not weak enough to force the Fed to ignore inflation. That leaves CPI as the near-term gatekeeper for whether lower yields are a constructive easing impulse or simply the first stage of a growth problem. The market implication is that the same payroll softness can be bullish or bearish depending on whether inflation gives the Fed room to treat it as cooling rather than stress. If inflation cools while claims stay contained, markets can price a better policy mix. If inflation stays firm while claims begin to rise, the Fed loses the clean path, because policy would be restrictive for inflation while growth risk is becoming more visible.

Bottom Line

The CPI Print Decides Whether Relief Can Broaden

The bottom line is that policy relief now needs confirmation. A cooler core CPI print would not make the Fed easy, but it would reduce the odds that policymakers have to lean harder against financial conditions. That is the path where duration can stabilize, equity breadth can improve, and leadership can broaden beyond the narrow sleeves that have carried the market. A hotter core print would not automatically mean recession, but it would force the market to price tighter policy risk at a time when valuations, credit, and leadership already depend on a benign inflation path. The difference between those two outcomes is why CPI matters less as a data release and more as the next regime test.
