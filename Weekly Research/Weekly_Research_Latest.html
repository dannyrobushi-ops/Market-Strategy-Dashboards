APEX INSIGHTAUGUST 15, 2026

The Consumer and the Cycle

Labor-market churn, household expectations, inflation sensitivity, and rate-sensitive demand are converging into a more conditional signal for the expansion.

KEY CONCLUSIONS

  • Labor conditions are softer in measures of churn and expectations, even while broad layoffs remain contained.
  • Headline index strength is not a substitute for durable market breadth beneath the surface.
  • Mortgage rates and energy costs keep the household and small-business outlook sensitive to financial conditions.
  • The cycle depends on whether slower demand relieves price pressure before it materially reduces income growth.
MARKET SIGNAL 01

Labor-Market Churn and the Jobless Rate

Over the past two weeks, we have received a significant portion of the economic data that sits at the center of the Federal Reserve's dual mandate of maximum employment and price stability. On the employment side, the Employment Situation report provides the most closely followed readings on Nonfarm Payrolls and unemployment, while CPI and PPI provide important signals on the inflation side of the mandate. In our previous note, we focused primarily on the deterioration developing beneath the surface of the labor market. This week, we want to extend that framework and connect it with the inflation data because the interaction between the two will ultimately determine the Fed's reaction function from here.

That reaction function has become increasingly important under Kevin Warsh because one of the defining characteristics of the new regime has been considerably less forward guidance. Rather than explicitly telling markets where policy is likely headed several meetings in advance, Warsh has signaled a preference for allowing incoming economic data to play a larger role in determining policy. That places greater importance on understanding not simply whether an individual payroll or inflation report is strong or weak, but how the combination of employment, wages, inflation, growth, and financial conditions changes the balance between the Fed's two mandates. The market is effectively being forced to estimate the reaction function itself.

The July Employment Situation report moved that balance somewhat further toward the employment side. Nonfarm Payrolls declined during the month, while May and June were revised lower by a combined 103K. Looking across the trailing twelve months, cumulative payroll revisions now total roughly 371K to the downside. While that sounds substantial, it has actually improved considerably from March 2025, when trailing twelve-month revisions exceeded one million jobs. The direction nevertheless remains important because persistent negative revisions tell us that labor demand looked stronger in real time than it ultimately proved to be once more complete establishment data became available.

This becomes particularly important around turning points in the business cycle. Payroll estimates have historically struggled when the underlying rate of business formation and destruction changes rapidly because models designed around recent historical relationships are inherently slow to recognize regime shifts. The BLS birth-death adjustment attempts to account for employment created by new businesses and lost through business closures that are not immediately captured by the establishment sample. That process works reasonably well during stable economic environments but can become less reliable around turning points, when business formation and failure rates change more quickly than historical relationships imply. This is one reason persistent downward revisions deserve more attention than any individual payroll miss, even though revisions themselves do not establish that a recession has begun.

Despite the weakness in payroll growth, the unemployment rate remains remarkably contained. U-3 recently printed near 4.1%, but the underlying household data make that stability somewhat less reassuring than the headline suggests. Labor-force participation has continued declining, meaning fewer people are being counted within the denominator used to calculate unemployment. The unemployment rate can therefore remain stable, or even decline, despite weaker employment if workers leave the labor force quickly enough. This is increasingly what we have seen. The deterioration in labor supply has helped offset the combination of layoffs and workers entering the labor force but failing to find employment, allowing U-3 to remain relatively stable even as the underlying hiring environment has weakened considerably.

This also helps explain why estimates of the payroll growth required to keep unemployment stable have fallen so dramatically. With population growth and labor-force participation slowing, some estimates now place the breakeven pace of monthly payroll creation below 30K. In other words, the economy no longer needs the 150K to 200K monthly payroll gains investors became accustomed to during earlier stages of the expansion simply to prevent unemployment from rising. That makes today's low payroll numbers less recessionary than they would have been historically, but it also means the labor market has considerably less cushion if employment growth deteriorates further.

The chart below adds another important dimension through the quits rate. The quits rate measures the percentage of workers voluntarily leaving their jobs and therefore provides a useful read on worker confidence and labor-market dynamism. Employees are considerably more willing to quit when they believe another job can be found easily and considerably less willing when outside opportunities become scarce. Not surprisingly, the quits rate has historically maintained a close relationship with wage growth because workers changing jobs are often able to negotiate larger compensation increases than workers remaining with their existing employer.

The decline in quits from the extraordinary post-pandemic peak near 3% was initially exactly what the Federal Reserve wanted to see. The labor market of 2021 and 2022 was characterized by enormous excess demand for workers, unusually high job switching, accelerating wages, and businesses competing aggressively for a limited supply of available labor. Falling quits helped unwind that imbalance and became an important component of the subsequent disinflation process. Less worker turnover reduced bargaining power, slowed job-switcher wage growth, and lowered the probability that the initial inflation shock would remain embedded through a persistent wage-price feedback loop.

The quits rate has now fallen to roughly 2%, however, and the interpretation becomes more complicated from here. The normalization that was previously helping the inflation story is increasingly approaching levels where additional deterioration would begin raising questions about labor-market health. As shown below, sustained declines in quits toward and below current levels have historically been associated with rising unemployment because workers become progressively less willing to voluntarily leave existing positions as outside opportunities disappear. The signal is not that a 2% quits rate automatically implies recession. Rather, it tells us that much of the beneficial normalization from the post-pandemic extreme has already occurred, leaving less room for quits to decline further without beginning to send a more defensive message about employment.

That creates an unusually useful two-sided indicator for the Fed's reaction function. If quits begin rising meaningfully from here while job openings and hiring strengthen, the inflation side of the mandate becomes more important again. Greater worker confidence would likely place upward pressure on job-switcher wages and eventually broader compensation growth, increasing the risk that inflation becomes more persistent through labor costs. If quits instead continue falling while payroll growth, employment diffusion, and participation deteriorate simultaneously, the signal shifts toward the employment side of the mandate. At that point, declining quits would no longer represent welcome labor-market normalization. They would increasingly indicate that workers are becoming reluctant to leave jobs because alternative employment opportunities are disappearing.

That is why the chart below may be particularly useful over the next several months. The quits rate is effectively sitting near an inflection point between two different macro interpretations. A sustained move higher would tell us to pay closer attention to wages and the possibility that labor-market tightness is rebuilding, particularly if inflation remains elevated. A sustained move lower would tell us to focus increasingly on unemployment, claims, hiring, and household income as evidence that the labor slowdown is becoming more durable. Neither outcome is predetermined today. What has changed is that the quits rate has already completed much of the normalization the Federal Reserve needed from its pandemic extreme.

This reinforces the broader argument we have made throughout the past several reports. The labor market has moved from overheated, to balanced, and increasingly toward the boundary where additional cooling becomes less obviously constructive. The Fed wanted fewer openings, lower quits, slower wage growth, and more moderate payroll creation because those developments reduced inflation pressure without requiring mass layoffs. Much of that adjustment has now occurred. The policy challenge from here is that the same indicators that helped deliver disinflation can become warning signs if they continue moving in the same direction. The reaction function therefore becomes increasingly asymmetric. The Fed still needs confidence that wage and price pressures are contained, but it can no longer assume that every additional increment of labor-market cooling is benign.

MARKET SIGNAL 02

Worker Confidence and the Volatility Surface

Continuing with the quits rate, its usefulness extends beyond the labor market and inflation. Because quits provide a real-time read on workers' willingness to voluntarily leave existing employment, they also contain information about confidence in the broader economic cycle. That makes the quits rate relevant for financial markets as well. As shown below, quits have historically maintained a meaningful inverse relationship with the VIX. Periods of elevated worker confidence and higher voluntary turnover generally coincide with relatively subdued equity volatility, while deeply depressed quits have tended to occur alongside much more volatile market environments.

The fundamental relationship makes intuitive sense. Workers generally quit when they believe another job is readily available and are considerably less willing to do so when employment opportunities become scarce. A falling quits rate therefore represents more than simply lower employee turnover. At sufficiently depressed levels, it can signal that workers perceive the balance of power in the labor market to have shifted materially toward employers. Historically, those environments have often coincided with rising unemployment, weaker household income growth, softer consumption, and increasing pressure on corporate revenues. The VIX is ultimately pricing uncertainty around the earnings and economic outlook, meaning deterioration in worker confidence and rising market volatility can be different expressions of the same underlying business-cycle stress.

The sequencing is important. Companies rarely move directly from slower growth to widespread layoffs. The adjustment typically begins with fewer job openings, slower hiring, reduced employee turnover, and declining worker confidence. If demand continues deteriorating, businesses eventually begin protecting margins by reducing labor costs, which for many companies represent their largest operating expense. Layoffs then weaken aggregate labor income, lower household consumption, and place additional pressure on corporate revenues. What begins as a slowdown in hiring can therefore become self-reinforcing once businesses begin shedding existing employees. Lower employment produces weaker income, weaker income weighs on consumption, weaker consumption pressures revenues and margins, and deteriorating earnings eventually feed back into additional cost reductions.

That is the point where the relationship with volatility becomes particularly important. Equity markets can generally absorb slower hiring when revenues and earnings remain intact. They have considerably more difficulty absorbing an outright firing cycle because employment losses directly attack the household income supporting roughly 70% of U.S. economic activity. As earnings expectations are revised lower and uncertainty around the depth of the slowdown increases, correlations across equities tend to rise and investors demand greater compensation for risk. The VIX therefore begins moving higher at precisely the point where a deteriorating labor market transitions from being helpful for monetary policy to harmful for corporate fundamentals.

Importantly, we are not at those levels today. The quits rate sits near 2.0%, while the VIX remains subdued near 14.2. The current labor market has been unusual for quite some time because hiring has slowed dramatically without producing the corresponding surge in layoffs and unemployment that would normally accompany such a large deterioration in labor demand. As discussed above, businesses have primarily adjusted through fewer openings and reduced hiring rather than widespread reductions in existing payrolls. That low-hiring, low-firing equilibrium helps explain why worker confidence has weakened while equity volatility remains remarkably contained.

The chart below should therefore be viewed as a framework rather than a timing signal. A 2% quits rate does not imply that volatility must rise, just as a low VIX does not tell us that labor conditions are healthy. What matters is the direction from here. If quits stabilize or begin rising while job openings, hiring, and wage growth improve, the labor market would be signaling renewed confidence, although that would also require greater attention to the inflation implications discussed above. If quits continue falling toward the levels historically associated with recessions while unemployment, claims, and permanent layoffs begin moving higher, the message changes materially. At that point, weaker labor-market confidence would increasingly signal deterioration in household income and corporate earnings rather than the benign normalization the Federal Reserve has been attempting to engineer.

MARKET SIGNAL 03

Index Strength and Internal Breadth

The equity market itself is not currently signaling the type of internal deterioration that would warrant becoming structurally defensive. The S&P 500 has returned to new all-time highs following a relatively brief pullback that was concentrated primarily within Technology and the AI-related names responsible for much of the market's prior advance. Several of those stocks experienced meaningful drawdowns after historic runs, while the unwind also exposed leverage within the ecosystem, including the highly publicized blowup at Situational Awareness. That episode understandably provided additional fuel for the argument that the AI investment cycle has become a bubble supported by circular financing, increasingly aggressive capital structures, and expectations that will ultimately prove impossible to satisfy. We are not arguing that those risks do not exist. As we have discussed repeatedly, the scale of AI capital spending, deteriorating hyperscaler free cash flow, rising depreciation expense, and increasingly demanding valuations deserve close attention. The distinction is that the market is not currently showing evidence that those concerns have developed into broader systemic stress.

The chart below is one reason we remain hesitant to make that leap. We pair the S&P 500 with the percentage of index constituents trading above their 200-day moving averages, which provides a simple measure of participation beneath the headline index. Since roughly May, breadth has improved in an unusually persistent fashion, moving almost in a straight line higher toward 75%. That means three out of every four S&P 500 companies are now trading above their long-term trend. Importantly, this improvement continued through a period when several of the largest Technology and AI-related companies corrected. Rather than weakness in the Magnificent 7 automatically dragging the entire index lower, capital rotated toward other sectors and allowed the broader market to absorb the decline. That is generally how healthy market churn is supposed to work.

This is a meaningful change from the environment we were discussing earlier in the year. At that point, Semiconductor and Technology leadership had become historically extended, a relatively small percentage of companies were outperforming the index, and the S&P 500 had become increasingly dependent on a narrow collection of AI beneficiaries to sustain index-level returns. The primary risk was that once those leaders corrected there would be insufficient sponsorship elsewhere to absorb the selling. Thus far, the opposite has occurred. Technology has consolidated while participation has broadened, allowing the market to digest an important leadership transition without producing significant index-level damage.

The behavior of volatility beneath the surface reinforces that message. Earlier in the summer, constituent volatility and dispersion were near unusually elevated levels even while the VIX remained subdued. Single stocks were experiencing large moves in opposite directions, and historically low correlations allowed those moves to offset one another at the index level. That was a potentially fragile equilibrium because a reversal in leadership combined with rising correlations could have quickly translated elevated single-stock volatility into much higher index volatility. Instead, constituent volatility and dispersion have recently declined alongside the VIX. In other words, the transition away from extreme AI leadership has occurred through declining rather than rising market stress. The market has effectively absorbed a meaningful rotation without requiring a volatility event to clear positioning.

That does not mean risk has disappeared. It means the source of near-term vulnerability has shifted away from market breadth and toward positioning. CFTC data continue showing S&P 500 positioning near the upper end of its historical distribution, with net-long exposure recently reaching approximately the 99th percentile before moderating somewhat, while VIX positioning remains near the opposite extreme below the 5th percentile. Investors are therefore heavily exposed to continued equity strength while carrying relatively little volatility protection. That creates an asymmetric positioning backdrop where the market can become vulnerable to a faster correction if a catalyst forces investors to reduce risk and rebuild hedges simultaneously.

Positioning by itself, however, is rarely the catalyst. Crowded markets can remain crowded for extended periods when earnings remain supportive and breadth continues improving. Something generally needs to change the underlying narrative before positioning becomes an active source of selling. Thus far, even several plausible catalysts have failed to accomplish that. The continuing conflict with Iran has increasingly been treated as background noise by equities, oil has struggled to revisit the highs reached earlier in the conflict, and the market has absorbed materially tighter financial conditions without producing sustained stress. Long-term interest rates remain near multi-decade highs, term premia have repriced materially higher, and the cumulative increase in the cost of capital should not be dismissed. Yet the equity market continues demonstrating that earnings growth and improving participation are currently sufficient to offset those pressures.

This is why breadth remains one of the most important indicators to monitor from here. A market at all-time highs with only a shrinking group of companies above their 200-day moving averages would send a considerably more defensive message. That is not today's configuration. The S&P 500 is near record highs while approximately 75% of its constituents remain above their 200-day moving averages, suggesting that the underlying advance is becoming broader rather than narrower. The correction in Technology therefore looks more like rotation than outright risk reduction so far.

The vulnerability would increase considerably if that relationship begins to change. If the S&P 500 continues making new highs while breadth rolls over, constituent volatility begins rising again, and positioning remains historically stretched, the ingredients for a more meaningful correction would begin accumulating. For now, however, the market has passed an important test. Its most crowded leadership group corrected, several high-profile AI names experienced significant drawdowns, and a leveraged participant within the ecosystem failed, yet capital rotated rather than disappeared. The market yawned because the broader tape was strong enough to absorb it. Until breadth begins telling us otherwise, the evidence continues to favor a broadening bull market with elevated positioning risk rather than the beginning of the systemic AI unwind that bears have been waiting for.

MARKET SIGNAL 04

Housing and the Long End

The consumer side of the economy continues to look considerably less constructive when viewed through the lens of affordability, and nowhere is that more visible than in housing. The chart below pairs Existing Home Sales with the 30-year mortgage rate and illustrates just how dramatically the housing market has changed since the Federal Reserve began tightening policy. From a long-term historical perspective, mortgage rates near 6.5% to 7% are hardly unprecedented. In that sense, mortgage rates have simply normalized after spending much of the post-Global Financial Crisis period at unusually depressed levels. The problem is that virtually nothing else about housing affordability has normalized alongside them. Home prices remain dramatically above pre-pandemic levels, household incomes have failed to keep pace with the increase in monthly ownership costs, and financing costs have reset higher simultaneously. The result is a combination of home prices, incomes, and mortgage rates that remains incompatible with anything resembling normal housing turnover.

Existing Home Sales tell that story clearly. Despite continued population growth and a much larger nominal economy, annualized sales remain near roughly 4 million units, around levels historically associated with recessions and major housing downturns. Yet this has occurred without the collapse in home prices normally associated with that degree of weakness in transaction volumes. Instead, the market has largely adjusted through quantity rather than price. Fewer households can afford to purchase homes at current monthly payments, while existing homeowners have very little incentive to sell properties financed at dramatically lower rates. The result is a housing market where buyers cannot afford to buy and many existing owners have little economic incentive to sell.

The mortgage-rate lock-in effect remains central to that dynamic. Millions of homeowners refinanced or purchased properties when mortgage rates were near 3%, effectively turning their mortgage into a valuable financial asset. Selling the home means surrendering that financing and replacing it with a mortgage carrying a rate more than twice as high in many cases. Even if a homeowner sells one property and purchases another at a similar price, the monthly payment can increase substantially simply because the financing structure has changed. That has constrained existing inventory, reduced mobility, and prevented the normal clearing mechanism through which weaker demand historically translated into materially lower home prices.

The lock-in effect, however, should not obscure the larger affordability problem. Even a substantial increase in existing inventory would not completely resolve the mismatch because the monthly payment required to purchase the median home remains elevated relative to household income. The housing market therefore increasingly requires some combination of lower mortgage rates, slower home-price appreciation, higher nominal incomes, or greater housing supply to restore equilibrium. Without movement across at least one of those variables, transaction activity can remain depressed for considerably longer than traditional housing-cycle models would suggest.

This matters for the broader economy because housing is not an isolated asset market. Turnover generates activity across mortgage lending, brokerage commissions, title services, insurance, moving companies, furniture, appliances, renovations, and a wide range of other consumer expenditures. A homeowner who remains locked into an existing property does not simply remove one transaction from the housing data. That decision eliminates a chain of economic activity that would normally accompany the purchase and sale of a home. Persistently depressed turnover therefore represents an ongoing restraint on portions of consumption and residential activity even if headline GDP continues to be supported by AI-related capital spending and other areas of the investment economy.

The affordability problem also reinforces the K-shaped dynamics we have discussed throughout this cycle. Existing homeowners who locked in low mortgage rates while home prices subsequently appreciated have experienced an extraordinary increase in housing wealth while keeping their monthly financing costs largely fixed. Younger households and first-time buyers face almost the exact opposite environment. They are attempting to purchase the same assets at substantially higher prices while financing them at substantially higher rates. The wealth created by the housing boom has therefore accrued disproportionately to households that already owned property, while the adjustment burden has fallen on those attempting to enter the market.

That divide increasingly extends beyond housing and into household formation itself. The ability to purchase a home has historically been closely connected with decisions surrounding marriage, children, geographic mobility, and long-term household formation. When the cost of establishing an independent household rises significantly faster than income, those decisions can be delayed. Sustained affordability pressure therefore has potential demographic consequences that extend well beyond the current business cycle, particularly if younger households increasingly delay homeownership and family formation because the economics simply do not work at prevailing prices and financing costs.

Housing affordability has consequently become an increasingly important political issue as well. The previous administration failed to materially resolve the underlying mismatch between housing supply, prices, incomes, and financing costs, and the current administration has yet to produce a solution capable of meaningfully changing that equation. The difficulty is that there is no painless policy lever. Lower mortgage rates would improve affordability but could simultaneously stimulate demand and place renewed upward pressure on home prices. Subsidizing buyers without increasing supply risks producing a similar outcome. Policies aimed at materially increasing housing supply are more likely to address the structural problem, but construction, zoning reform, permitting, infrastructure, and labor constraints mean that supply responds slowly.

This is also why the inflation discussion that follows becomes so important. Housing represents one of the clearest examples of why lowering the inflation rate is not the same thing as reversing the affordability shock households have already experienced. Even if shelter inflation continues moderating on a year-over-year basis, the underlying price level remains substantially higher than it was before the pandemic. Consumers do not experience affordability through the rate of change in CPI. They experience it through the monthly payment required to purchase a home relative to the income arriving in their bank account.

For now, housing remains less a recession signal than a structural constraint on the consumer. Existing Home Sales have already absorbed an extraordinary amount of weakness without pulling the broader economy into contraction because other sources of growth, particularly capital spending and the AI investment cycle, have filled part of the gap. But the chart below makes clear that the affordability problem has not been solved. Mortgage rates have normalized. Home prices and household incomes have not adjusted sufficiently around them. Until that equation changes, housing turnover is likely to remain depressed and the divide between existing asset owners and households attempting to enter the housing market will remain one of the defining features of the current economic cycle.

MARKET SIGNAL 05

Energy Prices and Small-Business Inflation

Inflation came in at just 0.1% month over month, reinforcing the argument that the Federal Reserve has little reason to raise rates again at the moment. Headline CPI is now running around 3.4% year over year, while core inflation has moderated toward roughly 2.5%. Neither measure is completely back to the Fed's 2% objective, but the direction of travel has become considerably more constructive. This becomes particularly important when paired with the labor-market discussion above. Payroll growth has slowed, participation has weakened, quits have normalized, and wage pressures are moderating at the same time underlying inflation is moving closer to target. The combination looks very different from the environment several months ago when resilient employment, rising commodity prices, and accelerating inflation allowed markets to begin pricing additional rate hikes.

A major contributor to the softer headline CPI print has been the reversal in energy prices. Oil fell sharply from the highs reached following the escalation in the Middle East, gasoline prices followed, and that decline ultimately flowed through into headline inflation. There is a strong relationship between short-term changes in crude and the direction of headline CPI because energy enters the consumer basket directly through gasoline and utilities while also affecting transportation, logistics, agriculture, chemicals, and the cost structure of businesses throughout the economy. That relationship works in both directions. The recent rebound in WTI toward approximately $85 suggests headline CPI could tick higher again over the next several reports as the energy impulse reverses. However, a hotter headline print driven predominantly by crude would not, by itself, establish that a new broad-based inflation cycle has begun.

That distinction between headline volatility and underlying inflation remains critical for the Fed's reaction function. Beneath the energy-driven swings, several important sources of inflation pressure continue moving in a more constructive direction. Auto prices have declined materially, insurance inflation has reversed from the extreme rates experienced earlier in the cycle, wage growth continues moderating, and softer labor demand makes it increasingly difficult for businesses to sustain aggressive price increases through higher labor costs. The inflation problem has therefore become less broad even though geopolitical developments can still create significant volatility in the headline data.

Oil also plays an important role in how households and businesses perceive inflation, which is not always captured by the measures economists prefer to emphasize. The chart below pairs WTI crude with the percentage of respondents in the NFIB Small Business Survey identifying inflation as their single biggest problem. The relationship is striking. As oil prices declined through 2025 and into early 2026, the share of small businesses identifying inflation as their primary concern fell from the mid-20% range toward roughly 11%. When crude subsequently surged above $100 following the Middle East escalation, inflation concerns quickly reversed higher, eventually reaching roughly 21%. As oil retreated, those concerns fell back toward 14%.

That relationship makes intuitive sense because small businesses experience inflation through actual operating costs rather than through the construction of an economic index. Fuel, transportation, utilities, packaging, chemicals, agricultural inputs, and other petroleum-sensitive costs affect margins directly. Smaller businesses generally have less ability than large corporations to hedge those exposures or absorb sustained cost increases, making commodity volatility particularly important to their outlook. The same mechanism operates for households. Consumers experience inflation every time they fill their gas tank, purchase groceries, pay an electricity bill, or finance a major purchase. As a result, oil prices can have an outsized impact on inflation psychology even when economists argue that the shock should eventually prove temporary.

This reinforces an important distinction between **inflation and affordability**. Inflation measures the rate at which prices are changing. Affordability reflects the cumulative price level relative to household income. Bringing CPI from 8% toward 3% is an enormous improvement in the inflation rate, but it does not reverse the increase in the price level that occurred along the way. After more than five years of inflation running above the Federal Reserve's target for much of the period, households are still purchasing necessities from a permanently higher starting point. This is why consumers can continue reporting severe affordability pressure even while economists correctly point out that inflation has fallen substantially from its peak.

The housing discussion above is perhaps the clearest example. A household attempting to purchase a home does not care that shelter inflation has slowed on a year-over-year basis if the home itself costs materially more than it did several years ago and the mortgage financing that purchase carries a rate near 6.5% to 7%. The same logic applies to food, insurance, automobiles, utilities, and other necessities. The rate of change can improve considerably without restoring the purchasing power that was lost during the inflationary period.

This also highlights the economic cost of the continuing Middle East conflict. Whatever the geopolitical rationale, the decision to enter a conflict in one of the world's most important energy-producing regions introduced another commodity shock at precisely the moment affordability remained one of the most important concerns for households and small businesses. The chart below illustrates how quickly that shock translated back into inflation concerns. Higher oil prices effectively function like a tax on consumers, and that tax is regressive because energy and food represent a considerably larger share of spending for lower-income households. The lower half of the K-shaped economy therefore absorbs a disproportionate share of the damage.

The Federal Reserve is increasingly forced to navigate the difference between these two problems. Policymakers have repeatedly emphasized that inflation has remained above target for too long, and there is understandable concern that allowing another commodity shock to become embedded in expectations could undo some of the progress made over the past several years. However, raising interest rates does not produce additional barrels of oil, reopen disrupted supply routes, or directly solve the affordability problem created by the cumulative increase in prices. In fact, additional tightening can compound that affordability problem by increasing borrowing costs for mortgages, automobiles, credit cards, and small businesses precisely when households are already absorbing higher energy and food costs.

This is why the source of inflation matters as much as the headline number. A reacceleration driven by stronger wages, accelerating demand, rising quits, and broad-based service inflation would give the Fed a fundamentally different problem than a temporary increase caused primarily by another oil shock. The former suggests excess domestic demand that monetary policy can reasonably address. The latter represents a supply constraint where additional tightening risks weakening employment and consumption without materially resolving the source of the price increase.

Taken together with the labor data above, the bar for another Fed hike therefore appears considerably higher than it did earlier in the year. Labor demand is cooling, wage pressures are moderating, and underlying inflation is moving closer to target even though oil remains capable of generating considerable volatility in headline CPI and inflation expectations. The Fed cannot ignore another sustained move higher in energy prices, particularly after several years of above-target inflation, but it also cannot treat every oil-driven increase in headline CPI as evidence that domestic demand needs additional restraint. The policy challenge is increasingly about preventing temporary supply-driven inflation from becoming embedded without unnecessarily tightening into a labor market that is already losing momentum.

For markets, that distinction will determine whether another rise in headline inflation represents renewed monetary tightening or simply volatility around an improving underlying trend. If oil stabilizes and labor continues cooling gradually, the Fed has room to remain patient while inflation moves lower underneath the headline noise. If oil accelerates again while quits, payrolls, and employment breadth continue deteriorating, policymakers would face a much more difficult combination of weakening growth and elevated inflation. That is the stagflationary tail risk worth monitoring. For now, however, the underlying data argue that the inflation problem is becoming more manageable even as the affordability problem remains very much unresolved.

MARKET SIGNAL 06

Household Expectations and Labor Anxiety

This brings us to the final chart in this piece and, in many ways, it ties together the broader tension running through the data discussed above. The aggregate economy continues to expand, the stock market remains near all-time highs, corporate earnings remain supportive, and inflation has moderated considerably from its peak. Yet the way households perceive their own economic situation looks dramatically different. The University of Michigan Consumer Sentiment Survey captures that disconnect across a wide range of questions, but the two series below are particularly useful: consumers' personal financial outlook and the percentage expecting higher unemployment. The deterioration is striking. Personal financial expectations have fallen toward the lowest levels in the history of the series, while roughly 62% of respondents now expect unemployment to increase. Historically, those two conditions have been much more closely associated with periods of meaningful economic stress than with an economy still expanding and an equity market sitting near record highs. That does not mean the survey is forecasting an imminent recession. Sentiment data can be influenced by politics, media coverage, geopolitical uncertainty, and other factors that do not necessarily translate directly into economic behavior. However, dismissing the weakness entirely because the hard data remain resilient would miss an important part of the current economic environment. Much of the disconnect comes back to the distinction between the aggregate economy and the household experience. Corporate America can tolerate 3% inflation considerably better than the average consumer when nominal revenues are rising, margins remain healthy, and businesses possess the ability to pass portions of their higher costs through to customers. Asset owners have also benefited enormously from rising equity and home prices. For households without meaningful financial assets, however, the experience has been very different. The cumulative increase in housing, food, insurance, energy, automobiles, and financing costs remains embedded in household budgets even though the rate of inflation has slowed. That is why improving CPI does not necessarily translate immediately into improving sentiment. Consumers experience price levels rather than year-over-year rates of change. A household does not feel materially better because groceries that increased dramatically over the previous several years are now rising at only 2% or 3%. Nor does a prospective homebuyer necessarily benefit from slower shelter inflation when home prices remain elevated and mortgage rates are still near 6.5% to 7%. The affordability problem discussed above therefore continues to weigh on perceptions even as the traditional inflation data move in a more constructive direction. The labor side of the survey adds another important layer. Expectations for higher unemployment have risen toward the upper end of their historical range even though the actual unemployment rate remains relatively contained. Some of that concern likely reflects the deterioration already visible beneath the surface of the labor market. Payroll growth has slowed materially, revisions have remained negative, job openings have normalized, the quits rate has fallen toward 2%, and employment breadth has moved closer to its breakeven level. None of those indicators currently signal mass layoffs, but they do describe a labor market where finding a new job has become more difficult and workers possess less bargaining power than they did several years ago. AI likely contributes to those concerns as well. Public discussion surrounding automation and potential job displacement has accelerated dramatically as companies increase AI investment and announce productivity initiatives. We believe some of the near-term fear that AI will eliminate broad categories of employment immediately is overstated. The aggregate data do not currently show widespread AI-driven job destruction, and the physical investment required to build AI infrastructure continues creating employment across Construction, Engineering, Manufacturing, power infrastructure, and related industries. However, consumer expectations do not need to perfectly reflect current reality to influence behavior. If households believe their future employment prospects are deteriorating, that belief can affect spending decisions today. This is where sentiment can become economically important even when it initially appears disconnected from the hard data. Consumption depends not only on current income but also on confidence surrounding future income. A household that expects to remain employed is more willing to purchase a home, finance a vehicle, travel, or make another large discretionary purchase. A household that becomes concerned about job security may delay those decisions, increase precautionary savings, or reduce discretionary spending even before income actually declines. Expectations can therefore become part of the transmission mechanism through which a softening labor market eventually reaches consumption. That becomes particularly important given the low savings cushion discussed throughout our recent work. The consumer has already absorbed several years of elevated inflation, affordability remains strained, and the savings rate remains low relative to historical norms. The upper half of the K continues to receive an important offset through the wealth effect as equity and housing values remain elevated. The lower half has considerably less insulation and depends much more directly on wages and continued employment. If confidence in future employment deteriorates while the cumulative cost of living remains elevated, the willingness and ability to continue spending becomes increasingly vulnerable. This does not mean weak sentiment must translate into weak consumption immediately. One of the defining features of the post-pandemic economy has been the extraordinary divergence between what consumers say in surveys and what they ultimately do. Asset appreciation, rising nominal wages, fiscal support, and a historically tight labor market allowed spending to remain resilient despite extraordinarily poor sentiment. That divergence can continue as long as income and employment remain intact. The risk is that the labor market is now providing less of that offset at precisely the point when consumers remain dissatisfied with their financial position. The chart below therefore summarizes the broader message of this report particularly well. From the perspective of corporate earnings and financial markets, the economy remains relatively healthy. Breadth within the equity market is improving, the S&P 500 remains near record highs, manufacturing and industrial activity remain constructive, and inflation has moderated enough to reduce the immediate need for additional monetary tightening. From the perspective of many households, however, the economy feels considerably less comfortable. Affordability remains poor, personal financial expectations have deteriorated sharply, and concern about future unemployment continues rising. The question from here is whether perception catches up to the resilient aggregate economy or the aggregate economy eventually catches down to consumer perception. If inflation continues moderating, oil stabilizes, employment avoids a firing cycle, and real wage growth gradually restores purchasing power, sentiment has considerable room to recover from historically depressed levels. That would reinforce the soft-landing scenario discussed throughout this piece and could eventually translate into stronger consumption as household confidence improves. The more concerning path is that deteriorating labor confidence begins changing behavior before inflation relief has meaningfully repaired household balance sheets. If consumers reduce spending because they increasingly fear unemployment, businesses would eventually experience weaker revenues, forcing them to protect margins through slower hiring and ultimately layoffs. That would create the feedback loop we have discussed throughout this report: weaker confidence leads to weaker consumption, weaker consumption pressures earnings, weaker earnings produce employment reductions, and rising unemployment further damages confidence. For now, that feedback loop has not begun in a meaningful way. The labor market is cooling rather than collapsing, corporate fundamentals remain broadly supportive, and equity-market breadth suggests investors are not positioning for an imminent economic break. But the chart below is a reminder that the margin for error on the consumer side has narrowed considerably. The hard data continue to describe an economy capable of expanding. Consumers increasingly describe one they are worried about participating in. Whether that gap closes through improving household confidence or deteriorating economic activity may be one of the most important questions for the remainder of the year.

CONCLUSION

What Matters for the Cycle

The broader picture is not one of an economy approaching an obvious breaking point, nor is it one where the remaining risks can be dismissed simply because equity markets are making new highs. Instead, the economy appears to be moving through the narrowest part of the normalization process. The excesses created during the post-pandemic period have largely been worked down. Labor demand has cooled, worker bargaining power has normalized, inflation has moderated, and the Federal Reserve has considerably less reason to add restraint than it did only several months ago. At the same time, none of those adjustments has yet developed into the synchronized deterioration across employment, earnings, and financial markets that normally defines the end of an expansion. That distinction leaves the Fed in a very different position from earlier in the cycle. The problem is no longer simply bringing inflation lower regardless of the collateral damage. Policy is increasingly about preserving the progress already made without pushing an economy with less labor-market cushion into unnecessary weakness. The remaining inflation risk is also increasingly dependent on its source. Renewed wage pressure and stronger domestic demand would justify a different response than another geopolitical shock to oil. With labor demand already moderating, tightening further in response to supply-driven inflation risks attacking the portion of the economy that monetary policy can influence while doing little to solve the portion it cannot. The same asymmetry exists on the consumer side. Lower inflation is gradually improving the flow of purchasing power, but it cannot reverse the cumulative affordability shock already embedded in household budgets. Housing provides the clearest example, but the issue extends across insurance, food, energy, automobiles, and financing costs. For households that accumulated assets before prices and interest rates moved higher, the wealth effect continues providing an important cushion. For households still attempting to accumulate those assets, particularly younger and lower-income consumers, the starting point remains considerably more difficult. That helps explain why the aggregate economy can remain healthy while perceptions of personal financial conditions remain historically weak. Markets, meanwhile, are currently siding with the aggregate data rather than the surveys. That deserves respect. The S&P 500 is near record highs, breadth has improved materially, and the correction in the most crowded AI leadership occurred through rotation rather than broad liquidation. The market has been given several opportunities to break and has instead continued absorbing tighter financial conditions, geopolitical uncertainty, and volatility within its largest leadership groups. Until participation begins deteriorating alongside price, it is difficult to argue that investors should become structurally defensive simply because positioning is extended or valuations are demanding. The more important question from here is therefore not whether the next payroll, CPI, or sentiment report is marginally stronger or weaker. It is whether the relationships between these variables begin changing. A labor market that stabilizes around current levels while inflation continues moderating would give the Fed room to remain patient and allow real incomes to gradually repair. A consumer that becomes more confident as affordability pressures ease would reinforce the expansion. Continued earnings growth accompanied by healthy market breadth would provide confirmation that the slowdown in hiring is being absorbed rather than transmitted through the rest of the economy. The warning signal would be synchronization in the opposite direction. If quits continue falling, unemployment begins rising, household confidence translates into weaker spending, and equity breadth deteriorates at the same time, the individual pockets of weakness discussed throughout this report would no longer be isolated. They would become parts of the same cyclical process. That is when slower hiring stops being disinflationary relief and begins becoming an earnings problem. For now, we are not there. The economy has slowed enough to reduce some of the inflation and policy risks that dominated the first half of the year, but not enough to break the expansion. Markets have broadened even as leadership has rotated, and the consumer remains cautious rather than capitulating. That leaves the current regime more balanced, but also less forgiving. The soft landing increasingly depends not on further cooling, but on stabilization. After several years in which policymakers needed the economy to slow, the next challenge may simply be knowing when enough is enough.
