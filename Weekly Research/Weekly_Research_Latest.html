Leadership Is Carrying The Risk

Technology leadership has become the main source of index strength at the same time the Fed is describing inflation risk as unfinished and the volatility surface is hiding more single-stock activity than the VIX implies.

July 11, 2026

Key Conclusions

  • Technology's 100-day relative return versus SPY is in a historically stretched zone at +22.4 percentage points. That is not a timing signal by itself, but it reduces the margin for error because the index is leaning heavily on one leadership complex.
  • The stretch is uneven. XLK is +21.8% above its 200DMA and SMH is +41.1% above, while IGV remains -4.2% below. The market is not simply rewarding all growth exposure; it is discriminating between AI infrastructure winners, software laggards, and defensive repair.
  • The June FOMC minutes shifted the policy conversation back toward inflation risk. The latest Apex Macro minutes score shows net hawkishness at 5.7 z-score versus the recent communications regime, which means easier financial conditions need better inflation confirmation to be validated.
  • The volatility tape is not as calm as the VIX suggests. VIXEQ is 50.0, DSPX is 47.4, and one-month implied correlation is only 4.0. Low correlation can suppress index volatility while single-stock risk rises underneath.

The Starting Condition

The market enters this period from a position of leadership that remains fundamentally strong, but no longer early. Over the last 100 trading days, Technology has outperformed the broader S&P 500 by a margin that sits near the upper end of its post-2010 historical distribution. Extremes in relative performance do not necessarily signal an imminent reversal, but they do alter the forward risk-reward profile. Once leadership becomes this extended, future returns become increasingly dependent not on whether the underlying thesis remains correct, but on whether investors are willing to continue paying progressively higher valuations while simultaneously absorbing increasingly optimistic earnings expectations and crowded positioning. Historically, leadership transitions rarely occur because the fundamental story suddenly deteriorates. More often, they occur because the market has already priced in much of the good news, leaving less room for positive surprises and a much smaller margin for disappointment. As a result, the market becomes increasingly sensitive to incremental changes in expectations rather than absolute fundamentals.

That distinction is important. We are not arguing that the technology sector, or the AI investment cycle more broadly, has become structurally impaired. The AI capex cycle remains one of the strongest secular investment themes in the global economy, and many of the companies supplying memory, networking equipment, power infrastructure, cooling systems, semiconductor manufacturing equipment, and electrical infrastructure continue to possess superior earnings visibility relative to the average cyclical company. The fundamental backdrop remains exceptionally strong, and there is little evidence that hyperscaler investment plans are slowing in any meaningful way. Recent management commentary still points toward another several years of elevated infrastructure spending as the industry races to build sufficient compute capacity.

The issue is that markets discount future fundamentals rather than current ones. Much of that superior earnings outlook has already been capitalized into prices. Technology is no longer simply benefiting from improving fundamentals. It is increasingly being asked to exceed already extraordinary expectations. At the same time, the macro backdrop has become less supportive of perpetual multiple expansion. Treasury yields remain elevated, term premia have repriced materially higher, and the Federal Reserve has shifted toward a more restrictive reaction function as inflation has proven more persistent than expected. The market is no longer pricing the aggressive easing cycle that investors expected only a few months ago, meaning the valuation tailwind that supported long-duration growth assets has become materially less powerful.

As a result, technology has become responsible for carrying an increasingly disproportionate share of index-level returns. The concentration of earnings growth, margin expansion, and capital flows within a relatively small group of companies has left the broader market more dependent on their continued execution than at any point since the early stages of the AI cycle. That does not necessarily mean leadership must fail, but it does mean the hurdle for further outperformance has become considerably higher. The market is transitioning from an environment where simply participating in the AI theme generated excess returns toward one where security selection, valuation discipline, and identifying the next beneficiaries of that capital spending cycle become increasingly important.

This argues for a transition from a pure momentum regime toward a more selective market. The opportunity set is likely to shift from simply owning the largest AI beneficiaries toward identifying where capital rotates next within the broader investment ecosystem. The physical infrastructure supporting AI, industrial beneficiaries, utilities, power equipment, and selected cyclical sectors may increasingly offer a more attractive balance between earnings visibility and valuation than the most crowded segments of the technology complex. The AI story itself has not changed. What is changing is where investors are likely to earn the highest marginal return from that story.

Where The Stretch Lives

The cross-section of the market tells a much more informative story than the headline indices alone. While the S&P 500 remains near its highs, leadership has become increasingly concentrated within a handful of industries. Semiconductors and Technology continue to trade at some of the most extended levels in the market, while Software remains below its 200-day moving average and Healthcare has quietly repaired its technical structure without reaching similarly stretched conditions. That distinction is important because it highlights that investors are not indiscriminately chasing growth. Rather, they continue allocating capital toward a very specific subset of the AI ecosystem, leaving many other sectors and industries well behind. The market has broadened modestly beneath the surface, but leadership remains highly selective rather than universal.

Software is the clearest example of why that selectivity matters. The group did not lag simply because investors abandoned growth. It lagged because AI introduced a more complicated question for traditional software business models. Coding agents, Copilot-style products, Claude Code, Codex, and broader automation tools have made investors question whether seat growth, workflow lock-in, and pricing power are as durable as they appeared during the last software cycle. At the same time, the market has not yet fully rewarded software companies for potential AI monetization because the revenue model remains less direct than it is for semiconductors, power equipment, networking hardware, and data-center infrastructure. That leaves software in a very different position from the physical AI chain. It can become a rotation candidate if investors begin to see evidence of margin expansion, productivity capture, or better AI-enabled product adoption, but it still needs proof that AI is a net earnings tailwind rather than a disruption risk.

That matters because concentrated leadership cycles rarely unwind in only one fashion. The more constructive outcome is a rotation, where the most extended winners consolidate while capital gradually migrates toward sectors with improving fundamentals but less demanding valuations. In that environment, broader participation allows the index to continue advancing even as leadership changes beneath the surface. The less constructive outcome is a reversal, where the same stocks responsible for carrying index-level returns begin losing momentum while the rest of the market lacks sufficient earnings growth, positioning, or investor sponsorship to absorb the baton. Given the concentration that has developed throughout this cycle, the difference between those two outcomes becomes increasingly important for index-level performance.

This is precisely why monitoring both the distance from the 200-day moving average and the tail-frequency framework provides a more complete picture than looking at price alone. The distance-from-trend analysis identifies where prices have become extended relative to their longer-term trend, while the tail-frequency work measures how unusual those extensions are relative to each ETF's own historical distribution. In other words, it distinguishes between sectors that are simply trending well and those trading at historically rare extremes. Technology and Semiconductors now fall firmly into the latter category. They are not merely above trend, but occupying upper-tail regions that have historically been associated with increasingly asymmetric forward return profiles.

That does not imply those sectors cannot continue outperforming, but it does suggest that much of the positive fundamental outlook has already been reflected in price. By contrast, Software remains below its 200-day moving average despite being part of the broader AI ecosystem, illustrating that investors continue rewarding the companies benefiting most directly from the physical AI infrastructure buildout rather than treating growth as a single homogeneous trade. The market continues to discriminate aggressively between winners and losers. As a result, the relatively small number of sectors residing in these upper-tail extensions have become disproportionately important to both index returns and the broader market narrative going forward.

The Fed Constraint

The Fed minutes matter because they reinforce a shift in the way policymakers are beginning to view the AI investment cycle. For much of the last two years, AI has largely been discussed through the lens of productivity, corporate earnings, and long-term economic growth. Increasingly, however, it is becoming part of the Federal Reserve's reaction function. The Committee did not describe an economy suffering from weakening demand or deteriorating labor market conditions. Instead, the minutes characterized economic activity as remaining solid, labor market conditions as broadly balanced, and inflation risks as continuing to lean to the upside. Policymakers noted that "price pressures had become more broad based," that inflation risks remained "tilted to the upside," and that several supply-side forces could keep input costs elevated. In other words, the Fed is no longer viewing AI solely as a future source of productivity gains. It is also recognizing that the unprecedented scale of investment required to build that future may itself be inflationary in the near term.

That distinction is becoming increasingly important for markets because it changes the relationship between earnings and monetary policy. Historically, stronger earnings growth has often coincided with expectations for easier financial conditions as inflation moderated and the Federal Reserve gained flexibility to support expansion. The current cycle is materially different. The same AI investment boom driving earnings revisions higher is also fueling record levels of capital expenditure, increasing demand for power generation, electrical equipment, semiconductors, networking hardware, engineering labor, and construction materials. Rather than easing inflationary pressures, portions of the AI buildout are adding to them. As a result, the very force supporting corporate earnings may also be limiting the Federal Reserve's ability to validate easier financial conditions.

The inflation language in the minutes was especially important because it was not confined to one narrow category. Officials discussed broad price pressure, persistent services inflation, commodity and supply disruption risk, and the possibility that AI infrastructure demand could continue pushing up technology-product and electricity costs. They also noted that medium- and longer-term inflation expectations were still broadly consistent with the 2 percent objective, which is why this is not yet an inflation-credibility problem. The market implication is subtler. The Fed can remain patient if expectations stay anchored, but it has less room to endorse easier financial conditions when the growth impulse itself may be contributing to the inflation constraint.

The labor discussion creates the other side of the reaction function. The minutes described the labor market as broadly balanced and acknowledged that it was "not currently a source of inflationary pressures," while also pointing to lower dynamism in job finding and job availability. That combination matters because it means the Fed is not facing a simple overheating labor market, but it is also not seeing enough labor weakness to justify a rapid pivot if inflation stays sticky. In market terms, that keeps the policy put further out of the money. The Fed can tolerate slower hiring if inflation expectations remain contained, but it has less room to ease into an equity-led loosening of financial conditions when the inflation side of the mandate is still described as unfinished.

This creates one of the more important macro tensions facing markets today. The highest-duration segments of the equity market typically benefit most when two conditions exist simultaneously: earnings expectations continue moving higher while discount rates move lower. Today, only one side of that equation appears firmly in place. Earnings expectations remain exceptionally strong, but Treasury yields remain elevated, term premia have repriced higher, and the Federal Reserve continues signaling that inflation remains the primary policy constraint. The market has already moved a considerable distance from pricing an aggressive easing cycle to debating whether rates remain restrictive for significantly longer than previously expected.

None of this implies that the AI investment cycle is ending or that policymakers are preparing to aggressively tighten policy further. Rather, it suggests the hurdle for multiple expansion has become materially higher than it was during the early stages of the AI rally. As long as capital spending remains this robust and growth continues surprising to the upside, the Federal Reserve has little incentive to ease financial conditions prematurely. That leaves equity leadership increasingly dependent on continued earnings delivery rather than expanding valuation multiples. The AI story remains intact. The macro environment surrounding that story has become considerably more complex, and that complexity narrows the path for the type of broad multiple expansion that characterized the earlier phase of this cycle.

Market ReadLeadership can continue if earnings breadth improves and inflation pressure fades. The risk is that tech keeps carrying the index while the Fed keeps describing the same strength as a reason to remain restrictive.

The Volatility Surface Is Hiding The Stress

The volatility backdrop continues to send two very different messages depending on where investors choose to look. At the index level, conditions appear remarkably calm. The VIX remains subdued, implying that investors expect relatively limited movement in the S&P 500 over the coming month. Viewed in isolation, that would suggest a stable market with little evidence of broad-based stress. Beneath the surface, however, the picture looks materially different. Both constituent volatility and dispersion have climbed toward the upper end of their one-year ranges, indicating that individual stocks are experiencing much larger moves even as the index itself remains relatively stable. In effect, the market has become increasingly differentiated. Winners continue separating from losers, sectors are responding very differently to the evolving macro backdrop, and stock-specific fundamentals are playing a much larger role in determining returns than broad market beta.

The mechanism behind this divergence is correlation. The VIX measures expected volatility for the S&P 500 as a single portfolio, not the volatility of the individual companies that comprise it. When correlations are low, large moves in individual stocks tend to offset one another, allowing the index itself to remain relatively stable despite considerable activity beneath the surface. This has become increasingly evident throughout the AI cycle. Capital has continued flowing aggressively toward a relatively small group of companies benefiting directly from AI infrastructure spending, while other sectors have either consolidated or quietly repaired their technical structure. The result has been elevated single-stock volatility without a corresponding increase in index volatility.

On balance, this remains a constructive backdrop for active management and security selection because dispersion creates a wider opportunity set for investors capable of identifying improving fundamentals. However, it should not be confused with an absence of market risk. In many respects, low index volatility combined with elevated constituent volatility reflects the concentration dynamics discussed throughout this report. The index remains stable because leadership continues carrying an outsized share of returns while weakness elsewhere is largely offset. That equilibrium becomes considerably more fragile once leadership begins changing.

The risk is that correlations begin rising at precisely the same time the concentrated leadership group starts losing momentum. If the largest contributors to index performance begin correcting together rather than independently, the diversification currently keeping the VIX subdued begins to disappear. What initially appears to be a calm market can transition into a much more volatile environment as the offsetting mechanism weakens and individual stock moves become increasingly synchronized. Historically, this is often how volatility regimes change. They do not necessarily begin with the VIX moving higher first. Rather, they begin with elevated dispersion, narrowing leadership, and gradually rising correlations before eventually manifesting as higher index-level volatility. That is why monitoring the underlying cross-section of the market often provides a more informative picture of evolving risk than the VIX alone.

Portfolio Implication

The broader implication is not that investors should abandon leadership simply because it has become extended. Strong leadership trends can persist considerably longer than valuation alone would suggest, particularly when earnings revisions continue moving higher and liquidity conditions remain supportive. Throughout much of the AI cycle, investors who attempted to fade leadership purely on valuation were consistently early. Rather, the more balanced conclusion is that the market has entered a stage where leadership alone is unlikely to be sufficient to carry the entire index indefinitely. From here, the market increasingly requires either rotation or confirmation.

Rotation would represent the healthier outcome, allowing Technology and Semiconductors to consolidate after an extraordinary period of outperformance while leadership broadens toward areas such as Software, Healthcare, Industrials, Financials, or other cyclical sectors where expectations remain considerably less demanding. Confirmation, on the other hand, would require incoming macro data to validate current valuations by showing enough progress on inflation to ease pressure on the Federal Reserve without simultaneously weakening the earnings outlook that has supported equity markets throughout the AI investment cycle. Either path would provide a healthier foundation for the next leg higher than simply asking the same handful of companies to continue carrying an increasingly disproportionate share of index performance.

Without one of those two outcomes, the market becomes progressively more dependent on a narrow group of leaders continuing to absorb every source of macro pressure simultaneously. Elevated Treasury yields, higher term premia, persistent inflation concerns, tighter financial conditions, and increasingly demanding earnings expectations all become concentrated within the largest constituents of the index. That does not necessarily signal an imminent reversal, but it does mean the margin for disappointment continues shrinking as expectations rise.

Taken together, this leaves the market operating within a considerably less forgiving regime than the one that characterized the early stages of the AI rally. The low level of the VIX should not be interpreted as evidence that risk has disappeared. Rather, it reflects the fact that index-level volatility remains contained because correlations across stocks remain unusually low. Beneath the surface, however, dispersion continues running near the upper end of its recent range, leadership remains highly concentrated, and single-stock volatility has increased materially. The market is becoming increasingly selective, rewarding companies with improving earnings visibility while penalizing those where expectations are beginning to outpace execution.

That combination creates both opportunity and risk. Elevated dispersion generally favors active management because stock selection matters considerably more when correlations remain low. At the same time, it makes passive index stability increasingly fragile. The current equilibrium depends on leadership remaining intact while the rest of the market gradually broadens participation. The next major test for equities is whether that broadening can occur without reigniting the very inflationary pressures that have become embedded within the Federal Reserve's reaction function. If inflation continues moderating while earnings remain resilient, leadership can evolve into a healthier, more diversified bull market. If not, the market risks becoming increasingly reliant on a shrinking group of companies to justify historically elevated valuations at a time when monetary policy is no longer providing the tailwind it once did.

Bottom LineThe market can keep advancing, but the quality of the advance now matters more than the level of the index. Leadership is carrying more of the risk, the Fed is less willing to validate easy financial conditions, and the volatility surface says the calm is coming from low correlation rather than low single-stock activity.
